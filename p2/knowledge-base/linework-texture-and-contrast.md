# Linework, texture and contrast in black and white

Reference for how a design is rendered, in either mode. Every output is pure black ink on a pure white ground — no colour, no tint, no grey wash. Tone is produced by mark density, never by pigment.

## Working without colour

Colour normally carries three jobs at once: separating one element from another, creating depth, and setting mood. In monochrome all three fall to **linework, texture and contrast**, and each has to be chosen deliberately rather than inherited from a colour idea.

A brief written in colour terms still contains usable information. Translate it rather than discarding it:

- A dark, heavy, saturated colour brief means **high** contrast and **bold** or **solid-fill** treatment.
- A pale, washed, faded brief means **low** contrast and **fine** linework.
- A brief describing "depth" or "richness" means layered texture — **cross-hatching** or **stippling** — not a darker ink.

## Line weight

**fine** — hairline. Delicate, detailed, and it reads as texture rather than as drawing at any distance. It rewards close viewing and disappears on anything seen across a room. Poor on a back panel or a hem; excellent on a neckline or a cuff. Fine lines packed closely produce mid-tone.

**medium** — the default. Reads as drawing at conversational distance and holds up when reduced. Correct when the brief gives no signal.

**bold** — graphic, heavy, poster-like. The silhouette dominates and interior detail is largely lost. Reads at distance and in motion, which makes it right for a back, a hem, or anything seen briefly. Combined with **dense** it approaches solid black, which is usually not what a brief asking for "bold" wants — check that the design still has ground showing.

## Texture technique

**flat** — no interior modelling. Shapes are outlines or plain fills, with tone coming only from the balance of black and white areas. Clean and graphic. Suits geometric work, modernist and deco styles, and anything meant to reproduce cheaply.

**hatching** — parallel lines producing tone by their spacing. The classic engraving mark. Direction carries meaning: hatching that follows a form describes its curvature, hatching that ignores it flattens the form deliberately. Closely spaced fine hatching gives a smooth mid-grey; widely spaced bold hatching stays visibly linear.

**cross-hatching** — two or more hatching directions layered. Produces darker tone than hatching alone and a richer, denser surface. The angle between layers matters: a near-right angle reads mechanical, an oblique crossing reads hand-drawn. Adding layers is how depth is built without colour.

**stippling** — tone from dots rather than lines. Softer and more gradual than hatching, with no directional bias, so it describes rounded and organic forms well. It is slow to read at small scale and can vanish entirely when reduced. Best at medium and large scale.

**silhouette (solid-fill)** — shapes as solid black masses with no interior detail. The most reductive treatment and the most legible. Everything depends on the outline, which makes subject choice critical: a form that is not recognisable from its outline alone fails completely. Blackwork, woodcut and stencil traditions all work this way.

## Contrast level

Contrast describes the *spread* between the lightest and darkest areas, not the darkness of the ink.

**high** — large areas of pure black against large areas of pure white, with little in between. Bold, graphic, high impact, legible at distance. It is the natural partner of **solid-fill** and **bold**.

**medium** — a full range from white through mid-tones to black. The most versatile and the most conventionally "illustrative". The default when nothing suggests otherwise.

**low** — everything sits in a narrow tonal band, typically mid-greys built from fine marks, with no true black and little pure white. Subtle, textural, quiet. It reads as a ground rather than as a design, which is right for a secondary fabric and wrong for a statement piece. Low contrast with **bold** linework is close to contradictory: bold marks create black, and black creates contrast.

## Combinations that work

- **flat** + **bold** + **high** — poster graphics, modern geometrics, deco.
- **hatching** + **medium** + **medium** — botanical illustration, engraving, toile.
- **cross-hatching** + **fine** + **medium** — dense antique engraving, rich and detailed, wants **large** scale to be legible.
- **stippling** + **fine** + **low** — soft, textural, atmospheric grounds.
- **solid-fill** + **bold** + **high** — blackwork, woodcut, stencil, silhouette work.

## Combinations to avoid

- **stippling** at **small** scale — the dots merge into flat grey and the technique is wasted.
- **cross-hatching** with **low** contrast — the layers are what build tone, so suppressing tone removes the reason for the technique.
- **fine** linework on a design meant to be read at distance — a back panel or a hem.
- **solid-fill** with a subject whose outline is ambiguous — a silhouette can only say what its edge says.
- **bold** + **dense** without checking the ground — the result is a black rectangle.

## Style vocabulary

`style` is free text and carries a whole set of implications at once. A few common ones:

- **art deco** — geometric, symmetrical, stylised, hard edges. Favours **flat**, **bold**, **high**, and **block** or **mirror** repeats.
- **art nouveau** — organic, whiplash curves, asymmetric. Favours **medium** line weight and flowing **half-drop** or **toss** repeats.
- **william morris / arts and crafts** — dense interlocking botanicals, layered depth. Favours **cross-hatching**, **dense**, **half-drop**.
- **toile de jouy** — pastoral scenes drawn as fine engravings, widely spaced. Favours **hatching**, **fine**, **large**, **sparse**.
- **woodcut / linocut** — bold, reductive, visible tool marks. Favours **solid-fill** or coarse **hatching**, **high** contrast.
- **bauhaus / modernist** — pure geometry, no ornament. Favours **flat**, **high**, **block**.
- **japanese katagami / stencil** — flat shapes, negative space as subject, asymmetric balance. Favours **solid-fill**, **sparse**.
