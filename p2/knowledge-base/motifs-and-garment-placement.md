# Motifs and garment placement

Reference for designs in **motif** mode: a single design element, placed once, on one part of a garment. It does not repeat and is not required to tile.

## What a motif has to satisfy

A motif is read as an object, not as a surface. Three things follow from that:

1. **It has a silhouette.** Seen from across a room, before any internal detail resolves, it is a shape. If that shape is not recognisable and balanced, no amount of interior linework rescues it.
2. **It has an orientation.** Unlike a tile, it has a definite top, and it is seen the same way up every time.
3. **It is sized to a place.** The same motif is drawn differently for a cuff than for a back panel — not just scaled, but re-proportioned.

The most common failure is a motif designed as a tile unit: even internal density, no dominant silhouette, and no relationship to where it sits.

## Density and scale mean something different here

For a tile, `density` describes how motifs are distributed across a surface. For a motif there is only one, so `density` describes **internal** density — how much of the motif's own area is worked.

- **sparse** — open, linear, mostly ground showing through. Reads light and delicate. Suits fine linework and embroidery.
- **balanced** — worked areas and open areas roughly equal. The safe default.
- **dense** — heavily worked, close to a solid mass. Reads bold and graphic, and its silhouette carries almost the whole design.

`scale` describes the motif's size relative to the garment part it occupies, not an absolute measurement. **large** on a cuff is small in absolute terms.

## The garment parts

**neckline** — a curved band following the neck opening, symmetrical about the centre front. Seen close up and at eye level, so detail is worth having. The curve is the constraint: a motif designed on a straight axis distorts when it follows a neckline. Designs that work here read outward from the centre, or run as a continuous band around the opening.

**yoke** — the shoulder panel spanning the upper chest or upper back. Broad, gently curved, and interrupted by the shoulder seams. It carries a wide, shallow design better than a tall one. Symmetry about the centre matters because both halves are seen together.

**front** — the largest single field and the natural place for a focal design. Seen whole, at conversational distance. It tolerates a tall composition and complex internal structure. The centre front line matters: a design either sits symmetrically about it or deliberately ignores it, and half-measures read as an error.

**back** — as large as the front and seen from further away, usually with nobody looking at it up close. Bold silhouette matters more, fine detail matters less. A design that depends on close reading is wasted here.

**sleeve** — a long, narrow field that wraps, so the design is never seen flat and never entirely at once. It curves away from the viewer on both sides. Vertical or spiralling compositions work; wide horizontal ones disappear around the arm.

**cuff** — a short, narrow band at the wrist, seen in the round and often in motion. The tightest space of any part. Small scale, bold enough to read at a glance, and it must work as a band rather than as a picture: no single focal point, because there is no reliable "front" of a cuff.

**hem** — a horizontal band at the bottom edge, usually the widest continuous run on a garment and seen at a distance and from below. Suits repeating or continuous border designs. A single centred motif on a hem tends to look stranded.

**panel** — a defined insert or section without a fixed shape or position. The least constrained and the least informative: treat it as a self-contained field and let the motif's own proportions lead.

## Proportion by part

| Part | Field shape | Read at | Favours |
|---|---|---|---|
| neckline | curved, narrow | close | outward or banded, symmetrical |
| yoke | wide, shallow | close | wide, symmetrical |
| front | large, tall | conversational | focal, complex |
| back | large, tall | distant | bold silhouette |
| sleeve | long, narrow, wrapping | varies | vertical or spiralling |
| cuff | short, narrow band | close, in motion | small, banded, no focal point |
| hem | long horizontal band | distant, from below | continuous or repeating border |
| panel | undefined | varies | self-contained |

## When the brief names no part

Some motif briefs name no garment part at all — "a single peacock motif", "an embroidered crest". That is a complete brief, not a deficient one, and no part should be invented for it. Design the motif as a self-contained element with a strong silhouette and balanced internal structure, and let placement be decided later.

## Distinguishing motif from tile

The test is whether the design **repeats**, not whether the brief mentions a garment.

- "A seamless sleeve pattern" is a **tile**. It names a sleeve, but it describes a repeat.
- "An embroidered neckline" is a **motif** at the neckline.
- "A paisley print for a dress" is a **tile**. "A paisley placed at the yoke" is a **motif**.
- A brief that says only "a floral design", with nothing about repetition or placement, is a **tile** — the common case, and the reading that does not invent a garment part.

Techniques that imply a motif: embroidery, appliqué, a crest, a badge, a placement print, a screen-printed panel. Techniques that imply a tile: an allover, a repeat, a print, cloth described by the yard.
