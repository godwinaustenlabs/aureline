# Tile repeats and edge continuity

Reference for designs in **tile** mode: a seamless repeating unit laid edge to edge to cover cloth.

## What a tile has to satisfy

A tile is not a picture that happens to be square. It is one unit of an infinite surface, and it has to hold up under three conditions at once:

1. **Edges are continuous.** Anything crossing the right edge re-enters at the left at the same height; anything crossing the bottom re-enters at the top at the same position. A motif cut by an edge must complete on the opposite side.
2. **No secondary pattern emerges.** Repeating a unit hundreds of times makes accidental alignments visible that are invisible in a single swatch. This is the failure that ruins most otherwise good tiles.
3. **It reads at two distances.** Close up it is a set of motifs; across a room it is a texture with an overall tone. Both readings have to be intentional.

The single most common defect is a tile that looks correct alone and shows a grid, a diagonal, or a bare corridor once tiled.

## Choosing the repeat type

**block** — rows and columns align exactly. The simplest construction and the least forgiving: every motif sits directly above and beside another, so the eye reads strong horizontal and vertical lanes. Use it deliberately, for geometric and architectural work where the grid *is* the design — checks, tiles, formal damask layouts. Avoid it for organic motifs, where the grid fights the subject.

**half-drop** — alternate columns are offset downward by half a motif. The textile industry's default, and the right starting point when nothing in the brief says otherwise. The offset breaks the horizontal lanes a block repeat creates and makes the coverage read as continuous rather than stacked. It is the most forgiving repeat for florals, paisleys and any irregular motif.

**brick** — alternate rows are offset sideways by half a motif, like brickwork. The half-drop's transpose. It suits motifs that are wider than they are tall, and any subject that already reads horizontally — waves, borders, banded geometrics.

**mirror** — alternate rows are flipped to reflect the row above. It creates strong symmetry and a formal, heraldic feel. Its specific danger is that the mirror line generates shapes that exist in neither original motif: two facing halves can read as a face, a vessel, or a butterfly nobody designed. Check the axis, not the motif.

**toss** — no underlying grid; motifs scattered at varied angles but evenly distributed overall. The hardest repeat to execute and the most natural-looking when it works. "Evenly distributed" is the whole difficulty: a true random scatter clumps. Rotation should vary, and no two adjacent motifs should sit at the same angle.

## Scale and density together

Scale and density are not independent, and briefs routinely specify one while meaning both.

- **small** scale with **dense** packing gives a ditsy print: it reads as texture at any distance and the individual motif stops mattering. Suits secondary fabrics, linings, and anything meant not to dominate.
- **small** with **sparse** gives a spotted, airy ground. Large empty areas mean the ground itself becomes a design element, and the tile repeat becomes very visible — the eye finds the grid in the gaps.
- **large** with **dense** is the hardest combination to control. Motifs crowd, edges collide, and the unit is difficult to make continuous. It needs deliberate interlocking rather than placement.
- **large** with **sparse** is a statement print. One motif dominates and the repeat is obvious by design. The distribution has to be flawless because there is nothing to hide behind.
- **medium** with **balanced** is the safe default and the right answer when the brief gives no signal.

## Failures to design against

**Tramlines.** Motifs accidentally aligning along a diagonal, creating a visible stripe running through the surface. Almost always produced by a half-drop or brick repeat where motif spacing coincides with the offset. It is invisible in one unit and unmistakable across a bolt of cloth.

**Holes and clumps.** Uneven distribution in a toss repeat. The eye is drawn to the largest empty area, then to the densest cluster, and the pattern reads as a mistake rather than as scatter.

**Visible seam.** A motif that does not complete across an edge, or a background tone that does not match edge to edge. The most literal failure and the easiest to check: place the unit next to a copy of itself.

**Corner collision.** Four corners meeting create a junction that exists nowhere else in the tile. It needs checking on its own, because nothing in the single unit shows it.

**Directional lock.** A repeat where every motif points the same way can only be cut one direction, which makes the cloth wasteful to use. Varying rotation, particularly in a toss, avoids it.

## Reading the brief

- "Straight repeat", "full drop" → **block**
- "Drop repeat" → **half-drop**
- "Random", "scattered", "tossed" → **toss**
- "Ditsy" → **small** scale
- "Statement", "hero" → **large** scale
- "Allover", "packed" → **dense**
- "Airy", "open", "minimal" → **sparse**

A brief that names a period or movement usually implies a construction as well. Art deco implies geometric, symmetrical, often **block** or **mirror**. William Morris and the Arts and Crafts tradition implies dense, interlocking, **half-drop**. Toile implies large, sparse, scenic. Chintz implies dense florals. Ikat and shibori imply softness at every edge, which sits badly with fine linework.
